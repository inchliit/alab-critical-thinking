## Shared Socratic toolkit

Use questions to investigate a claim rather than to lead someone to a predetermined answer. Listen to the response and ask a relevant follow-up. Challenge the reasoning respectfully; give every participant time to contribute.

| Question type | Starting prompts |
|---|---|
| Clarification | What do you mean? Can you give an example? What exactly is the problem? |
| Assumptions | What are we taking for granted? What would challenge that assumption? |
| Evidence | What supports the claim? How reliable and relevant is it? What other interpretation fits? |
| Perspectives | Who might disagree? Whose experience is missing? |
| Consequences | Who is affected, how, and over what period? What could go wrong? |
| Questioning the question | Is this the right decision to frame? What are we trying to achieve? |

To formulate a question, identify a claim, select one of these six purposes, and ask an open question that could change your understanding. Avoid wording that assumes the desired answer. The historical generic question bank and formulation advice appear in conversation exchanges 5–6.

## Assessment used across cases

Each criterion is worth 0–4 points, for a total of 20: problem definition; evidence and uncertainty; Socratic questioning and alternatives; stakeholder consequences and feasibility; communication and reflection. A score of 4 is specific, well-supported, and attentive to limits; 3 is sound with minor gaps; 2 is partly developed; 1 is largely assertion; 0 is absent. For group work, record contributions so collaboration can be discussed in feedback. Different conclusions can earn the same score when their reasoning is equally strong.

## Case 1. AI product bias — the launch decision

**Provenance:** Historical conversation, exchanges 1–3 and 7–8; assistant-created teaching case.

### Scenario

You are a senior manager at a technology company developing an AI-driven product. Testing reveals a flaw that could produce biased outcomes for some groups of users. The proposed fix would delay launch by six months and substantially increase development costs. Investors are pressing the team to meet the original deadline. The case gives no measured rate or severity of harm, detailed budget, or verified remediation estimate.

### Objective

Use Socratic questions to examine ethical and business obligations, identify missing evidence, and defend a decision with conditions for review.

### Roles and materials

Groups of 4–5: facilitator/questioner, decision lead, investor advocate, user advocate, and optional observer. With four members, the facilitator also records observations.

Use this case sheet, a shared writing surface or document, and a timer. No additional research data is implied by the scenario.

### Activity mechanics

30 minutes total: 5 minutes to read, clarify the task, and assign roles; 15 minutes for Socratic discussion; 5 minutes to record a decision; 3 minutes for group reflection; 2 minutes for a facilitator wrap-up. The two-minute case review is included within the first five minutes. Whole-class reports require extra time or a sampled report-out.

### Original question bank — exchange 1

These questions preserve the original wording. Some contain assumptions worth challenging.

**Clarification Questions**

- What exactly is the flaw in the AI’s decision-making process?
- Can we quantify how often and how severely the bias affects the results?

**Probing Assumptions**

- Are we assuming that the bias will not cause significant harm to users?
- What are we assuming about the impact on our company’s reputation if the bias becomes public?

**Probing Rationale and Evidence**

- What evidence do we have that delaying the product would harm the company's market position?
- Do we have any data showing the potential financial impact of fixing the bias?

**Questioning Viewpoints and Perspectives**

- How might the affected users perceive the product if the bias is not fixed?
- What would our competitors do in a similar situation?

**Probing Implications and Consequences**

- What are the short-term and long-term consequences of launching the product without addressing the bias?
- Could there be legal implications if users experience harm from the biased AI?

**Questioning the Question**

- Why is the focus primarily on the deadline instead of user safety and fairness?
- Is this even the right question to be asking when considering the company's values?

**Probing Rationale and Evidence**

- What evidence do we have that delaying the launch to fix the bias will prevent reputational or legal risks?
- How do we know that our users will appreciate the delay rather than just wanting the product now?
- What examples of companies facing similar ethical dilemmas can we look to for guidance?

**Exploring Viewpoints and Perspectives**

- How might the investors react if we delay the launch? Are there ways to mitigate their concerns?
- What would the users’ perspective be on the bias if they were aware of it?
- How would our competitors handle this situation? Would they prioritize speed or fairness?

**Probing Implications and Consequences**

- What are the long-term implications for the company if we launch a product with known biases?
- Could delaying the launch create opportunities for competitors to gain market share?
- What are the potential consequences for customer trust and loyalty if we launch a biased product?

**Questioning the Question**

- Are we asking the right question when deciding between launching now or fixing the bias? Should we be considering other alternatives (e.g., partial launch or phased fixes)?
- Why are we focusing solely on the deadline versus product quality and fairness?
- Should the primary question be about short-term gains versus long-term sustainability?

## Case 2. Inclusive hiring — evaluating talent

**Provenance:** Historical conversation, exchanges 4 and 13; assistant-created teaching case.

### Scenario

A growing technology startup usually hires developers from a small group of prestigious universities. A candidate from a less prestigious school demonstrates innovative thinking and strong problem-solving ability. The team debates whether to continue its traditional approach or consider this candidate. Some colleagues associate the usual universities with excellence; others value a broader range of experiences. No comparative performance scores or mandatory licensing requirement are supplied.

### Objective

Examine a proxy for job performance, develop relevant selection criteria, and explain a fair evaluation process.

### Roles and materials

Groups of 4–5: hiring lead, skills assessor, traditional-practice advocate, candidate-experience advocate, and optional observer. Evaluate a fictional process; do not assess classmates or real applicants.

Use this case sheet, a shared writing surface or document, and a timer. No additional research data is implied by the scenario.

30 minutes total: 5 minutes reading and roles; 15 minutes questioning; 5 minutes drafting a selection process; 3 minutes reflection; 2 minutes wrap-up. This timing is a new teaching adaptation.

_Response: ___________________________________________

### Original question bank — exchange 4

These questions preserve the original wording. Some contain assumptions worth challenging.

**Clarification Questions**

- What is our current hiring practice, and why has it been successful in the past?
- What specific qualities make this candidate stand out from other applicants?

**Probing Assumptions**

- Are we assuming that graduates from top universities are always better employees? What evidence supports or contradicts this assumption?
- Is the concern about the company’s reputation based on objective criteria or subjective bias?

**Probing Rationale and Evidence**

- Do we have data showing a correlation between hiring from prestigious universities and employee performance?
- How do we define "success" for our employees? Is it based solely on their educational background, or are there other factors like creativity and problem-solving?

**Questioning Viewpoints and Perspectives**

- What might we gain by broadening our hiring pool? How could hiring from more diverse educational backgrounds impact our company culture?
- How might our traditional hiring approach limit our access to untapped potential?

**Probing Implications and Consequences**

- What are the long-term consequences of continuing to hire from the same universities?
- What risks do we take by hiring someone outside of our usual pool, and how can we mitigate them?

**Questioning the Question**

- Why do we equate a university's prestige with a candidate's potential value to the company?
- Is it possible that we’re focusing too much on the wrong criteria for evaluating talent?

## Case 5. Leadership Challenge at United

**Provenance:** PDF slide 44 assigns this case. Scenario and nine question themes adapted from the separate local two-page ALAB_LEADERSHIP AT UNITED.pdf; this reading was not embedded in the historical conversation.

### Scenario

The supplemental reading describes the 2017 United Flight 3411 incident. After passengers had boarded, seats were sought for four staff members. Volunteers were offered compensation and a later flight. When there were insufficient volunteers, passengers were selected to leave. Dr David Dao refused, citing obligations to patients, and airport security forcibly removed him; he was injured. Video circulated widely. The reading describes criticism of United’s response and an initial internal message defending procedures. Its claims about legal rights and market losses are historical source claims, not findings established by this teaching guide.
